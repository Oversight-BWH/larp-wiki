Arrest

An arrest is the lawful deprivation of a person’s immediate freedom for investigation or prosecution. An officer must have reasonable grounds to believe a crime has been committed and that the accused was involved.

Investigation

Investigators may gather witness statements, physical evidence, paperwork, and other lawful evidence. They must not fabricate, manipulate, or conceal evidence for the purpose of securing a conviction.

Charges

After investigation, a person can be formally charged. A charge should identify the offense, the legal authority, the relevant facts, and the actor bringing the case. The accused must understand the nature of the allegation.

Trial

A trial exists to determine whether the accused is legally responsible for the charged offense. It may include presentation of the case, evidence, witness examination, legal argument, and a judicial verdict.

  • Charges must remain tied to the specific alleged offense
  • Defense and evidence may be presented
  • Judges review law and facts, not rumors or bias
  • Trials must be focused and fair

Verdict, Sentencing, and Appeal

The court may return a guilty verdict, a not guilty verdict, a dismissal, or a mistrial. Where guilt is found, sentencing follows based on the seriousness of the offense, circumstances, aggravating factors, and mitigating factors.

A convicted person may appeal where a legal defect exists, such as judicial error, improper procedure, new evidence, or a sentence exceeding lawful authority. Appeals are not a license to re-argue every fact, but a review of whether the original law and process were properly applied.
