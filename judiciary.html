Threshold for Judicial Trial

Not every offense requires a full courtroom proceeding. Minor matters may be resolved administratively when the facts are clear, the punishment is within the authority of the enforcing officer, and no substantial dispute exists. However, serious or disputed matters must proceed to judicial review.

  • Administrative resolution for clear minor matters
  • Judicial review for disputes over facts or law
  • Mandatory judicial review for capital offenses
  • Failure to apply process is itself a legal wrong

Judicial Authority

Courts exist to interpret and apply the Law. Judges must consider statute, evidence, witness testimony, intent, aggravating factors, and mitigating circumstances. They must not substitute personal preference for legal principle.

Burden of proof

The prosecution must establish guilt through lawful evidence. A person must not be punished merely because they are disliked or politically unpopular.

Conflict of interest

A judge must recuse themselves when they have a substantial personal stake, relationship, or involvement in the case.

Punishments and Sentencing

Sentences must be proportionate and grounded in both the legal classification of the offense and the circumstances of the case. The Law recognizes aggravating and mitigating factors that may shift a punishment up or down.

Type Examples
Available punishments Warning, fine, confiscation, imprisonment, house arrest, exile, loss of office, corporal punishment, execution
Aggravating factors Premeditation, vulnerability of victim, abuse of authority, repeated misconduct, obstruction of justice
Mitigating factors Cooperation, confession, coercion, restitution, limited harm

Appointment and Removal

Judges and judicial officers must be appointed through lawful authority and selected for impartiality, competence, and integrity. They may be removed for corruption, bias, repetitive incompetence, criminal conduct, or direct violation of the Law.
